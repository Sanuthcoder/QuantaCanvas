1. Anatomy & Pressure Thresholds

A Crookes tube is an evacuated glass envelope containing two electrodes: a negative Cathode and a positive Anode.

At standard atmospheric pressure ($100 \text{ mmHg}$), high voltage fails to bridge the gap due to frequent collisions with gas molecules. Lowering pressure increases the mean free path $\lambda$ of ions.

2. Mechanism of Formation

At critical vacuum ($< 0.01\text{ mmHg}$), residual positive ions are violently pulled to the negative Cathode. Upon collision, they knock loose electrons via secondary emission.

These liberated electrons are accelerated by the intense electric field, shooting outward perpendicular to the cathode surface.

3. Travel in Straight Lines

To investigate the path of cathode rays, William Crookes placed a metal obstruction—a Maltese Cross—in their path.

Because the rays travel in straight lines and cannot pass through solid metal, they cast a razor-sharp shadow on the glowing phosphorescent screen at the back of the tube.

Cross Position

This experiment proved that cathode rays do not bend around obstacles like sound waves, behaving instead like rays of light radiating normally from the cathode.

4. Electromagnetic Deflection

Cathode rays carry a negative charge. Applying an electric field (plates) or a magnetic field (magnet) deflects the beam path.

J.J. Thomson used balanced perpendicular fields (Velocity Selector) to calculate the charge-to-mass ratio ($e/m$), proving they were fundamental particles: electrons.
